Do UMD Fraternity Members Actually Go to Class

Attendance, motivation, and what really keeps students showing up

By: Hayden Speace

On paper, college classes are simple, show up, take notes and learn. But for fraternity members at the University of Maryland, attendance is far from guaranteed.

A survey of about 30 UMD fraternity members shows that skipping class is not unusual. Most respondents reported missing multiple classes each week, with the largest group saying they skip more than three classes regularly. Only a small number said they attend all or nearly all of their scheduled classes.

The data suggests that while these students recognize the value of attendance, many make decisions based on what feels most useful for their time.

“I feel like I can learn the material faster on my own than sitting through a lecture,” one sophomore wrote in the survey.

That mindset appeared across many responses. While some described attending class as “very important,” others said it was only “somewhat important” or “not important at all,” especially in lecture based courses where slides and recordings are posted online.

Convenience plays a major role in those decisions. With access to recorded lectures and online materials, students said it is easier than ever to keep up without physically being in class.

“Usually because I’m too tired or I believe I can make up the work from home quickly,” another student said.

Fatigue was the most common reason students gave for missing class, followed closely by lack of motivation. Together, those factors were cited more than any others. Students also frequently mentioned studying for other classes, access to online materials and work schedules as reasons for skipping.

Jobs, in particular, appear to influence attendance. Several respondents reported working between 10 and 30 hours per week, with some working even more. Those students were more likely to miss multiple classes, often because of scheduling conflicts or exhaustion.

Still, attendance does not always align with academic performance. Many students who reported GPAs between 3.5 and 4.0 also admitted to skipping class regularly. That suggests some students feel confident managing coursework on their own, even if they are not attending lectures consistently.

Instead of relying on class time, many students said they catch up by reading slides, watching recorded lectures or getting notes from friends. However, not all students stay on top of missed material. Some admitted they do not consistently catch up after skipping, which could have long term effects on learning.

For some, the decision to skip comes down to how useful they believe the class actually is.

“I can stay home and study the same material as lecture on my own,” one student said.

Others were more direct, saying attending class had little impact on their grades, especially when assignments and exams were not tied closely to in person participation.

While the survey provides insight into fraternity student habits, it reflects a relatively small sample and is heavily made up of business related majors. Attendance patterns may differ across other programs at UMD.

Even so, the results highlight a clear shift in how students approach class. Attendance is no longer automatic, it is a choice influenced by energy, workload and whether students believe being in the room is worth their time.
